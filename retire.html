Retire in Manila.

The Philippines has the cheapest retirement visa in the region and almost nobody explains it properly in English. This does. It also covers the parts that will not appear in a brochure, including what happens if you die here, because that is the thing your family will have to deal with and nobody writes it down.

First, honestly

This works for some people and not for others.

Manila is not cheaper than Bangkok. It costs roughly the same and loses on traffic, air quality and walkability. If you are optimising purely for cost or for a beach, there are better answers and we will not pretend otherwise.

What Manila actually offers is narrower and, for the right person, much bigger: English everywhere, the easiest long-stay rules in the region, and a level of daily care and help that is simply out of reach on the same income at home.

The visa

Two routes, and most people pick the wrong one first.

Route one: the tourist extension. 30 days visa free, extendable in country up to 36 months in total. No deposit, no minimum age, no commitment. For a first year, this is usually the right answer, and it is how most people should test the idea before locking money up.

Route two: the SRRV, the Special Resident Retiree's Visa, run by the Philippine Retirement Authority. Indefinite stay, multiple entry, and a deposit you get back if you leave.

There are roughly 59,000 to 60,000 active SRRV holders, and under 10% are American, most of them former Filipinos. The PRA has said it is now actively courting Americans, Canadians and Europeans. You would not be a pioneer, but you would be early.

The thing nobody mentions: past 59 days on an extension you get an ACR I-Card, and that card is what unlocks a Philippine bank account and a full mobile wallet. Until you have it, your financial life here is limited. Plan for it rather than discovering it.

Healthcare

Good and cheap privately. Do not rely on anything following you here.

  • Medicare does not cover you outside the United States. Not for routine care and not for long-term care, which it does not cover anywhere. Medigap plans cover limited emergency care abroad, capped and only in the first 60 days of a trip. That is travel insurance, not a plan.
  • Private care in Makati and BGC is genuinely good and costs a fraction of US prices. Dental work commonly runs 50 to 70% less, medical procedures 40 to 80% less.
  • Public healthcare is a different system. Do not plan around it.
  • PhilHealth and most expat policies exclude long-term custodial care. SRRV holders pay into PhilHealth and still get no residential care benefit. Care is funded from capital, or from an indemnity long-term care policy that pays cash.
  • If you hold a US long-term care policy, find out now whether it is indemnity or reimbursement. Indemnity policies pay cash with no geographic restriction and work here. Reimbursement policies frequently do not. This one question can be worth thousands a month and takes ninety seconds to answer.
Help at home

The real reason people move here.

This is the part that changes lives and the part that has to be said carefully.

Live-in caregiver, Metro ManilaAbout US$400 to 450 a month
Licensed private nurse, live inAbout US$500 to 800 a month at market salary
The same coverage in the USUS$4,000 to 10,500 a month

The point is not that people are cheap. The point is that a Western pension buys a level of care at home here that is out of reach on the same pension in Ohio. The person doing that work should be paid at or above the local market rate, through a licensed agency, with benefits, and you should know what they earn. Anyone who will not tell you that number is not someone to hire through.

Two practical things. Round the clock care legally needs two people, not one, so any 24 hour price quoted as one salary is wrong. And under the Caregiver Welfare Act a worker must hold a TESDA certification, a health degree or a passed TESDA assessment to be a caregiver at all, whatever an agency calls them.

Housing

You cannot own land. You can get a very good deal.

  • Foreigners cannot own Philippine land. Not through a workaround, not through a friend, not safely.
  • You can own a condominium unit, but foreign ownership in any single building is capped at 40%.
  • You can lease land for up to 50 years, renewable for 25.
  • It is a buyer's and renter's market right now. Metro Manila residential vacancy is around 24.7%, roughly one unit in four, with a record 82,900 unsold units, close to three years of supply.
  • And the detail that matters most: asking prices are holding while real closing prices soften. The number on the listing is not the number. Do not pay asking, and do not take a photograph's word for anything.
The part nobody writes down

What happens if you die here.

We debated including this. We are including it because your family will face it, usually with no warning and no information, and because a guide that only tells you the good half is a brochure.

  • The death must be reported within 48 hours, and an unembalmed body must be buried within the same 48 hours.
  • If the cause is not obvious, an autopsy is mandatory and takes two to three weeks. Nothing moves until it clears. No burial, no cremation, no flight home.
  • Repatriation of remains runs roughly PHP 350,000 to 650,000. Cremation with ashes sent home, PHP 125,000 to 185,000. Local burial, PHP 75,000 to 95,000.
  • Your embassy pays none of it. It locates next of kin and issues a report of death. Every cost falls on your family.
  • Bank accounts are frozen the moment the bank learns of the death.
  • If you live with a partner you are not married to, she has no legal claim to the money, the remains, or the estate. Not some claim. None.

None of this is a reason not to come. It is a reason to spend one afternoon with a Philippine lawyer writing a local will, naming a local contact, and telling your family where the folder is. That afternoon is the single kindest thing on this page.

Before you decide

Four questions to answer honestly.

  • Have you spent a rainy August here? Two weeks in January tells you nothing about the year.
  • Is your income genuinely independent of the Philippines? Local wages are the other side of the arbitrage.
  • What is your plan if your health changes suddenly? Not if it declines slowly. If it changes overnight.
  • Who is your person here? Everyone needs one. If you cannot name them yet, that is the first job, not the flight.
