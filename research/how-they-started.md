# How the media companies started, and the Vaya project list

Date: 2026-09-06. From memory of well-documented company histories; numbers marked UNSURE where not verified in this session. Companion to `media-playbook.md` (what they do now) and `manila-revenue-model.md` (the video-to-revenue matrix).

## List 1. How they got started, and what they'd do if starting today

| Company | Year, place | The first move | How it got successful | What they'd do starting Vaya today |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Vice** | 1994, Montreal | A free community paper, "Voice of Montreal", run by Shane Smith, Suroosh Alvi and Gavin McInnes. Renamed Vice in 1996. | Free magazine paid for by brands that wanted youth credibility. Moved to New York in 1999. VBS.tv in 2007 with Spike Jonze as creative director. "The Vice Guide to Travel" (North Korea, Liberia) set the immersive format. YouTube channel in 2011, HBO show in 2013. Branded-content agency Virtue funded the journalism. | Go somewhere others won't and film it raw. Fund it with brand money, not audience money. Then over-expand and go bankrupt, so skip that part. |
| **Vox Media** | 2003 to 2005, US | One baseball blog, Athletics Nation, became SB Nation, a network of fan-run team blogs on shared software. | Jim Bankoff joined as CEO in 2008, raised money, and used the platform to launch verticals with proven editors who brought audiences: The Verge (2011, Josh Topolsky's team from Engadget), Vox.com (2014, Ezra Klein from the Washington Post). Vox video on YouTube from 2014. | Build a repeatable template and hire the person who already has the audience. One city page template, one host with a following. |
| **Eater** | 2005, New York | Ben Leventhal and Lockhart Steele's restaurant gossip blog for one city. | Went city by city, built the maps ("38 essential restaurants") that became the moat, sold to Vox Media in 2013. Later gutted by search changes. | One city, one signature list, then the next city. Never depend on Google for traffic. |
| **Thrillist** | 2004, New York | Ben Lerer and Adam Rich's daily email telling men in New York where to eat and drink. | City by city by email, then bought JackThreads and sold clothes to the list. | Start with an email, not a website. Sell the list something. |
| **The Infatuation** | 2009, New York | Two Atlantic Records marketers, Chris Stang and Andrew Steinthal, wrote restaurant reviews in the voice of a friend's text message. | Built an app, expanded city by city, bought Zagat in 2018, sold to JPMorgan Chase in 2021. | Write like the friend who already moved. Vaya's voice is already this. |
| **Time Out** | 1968, London | Tony Elliott's one-sheet listings of what's on in London. | Listings became the magazine, magazine became a global city brand, brand became Time Out Market food halls, which now carry the loss-making media. | Own the real-world layer early. The food hall outlived the magazine. |
| **Monocle** | 2007, London | Tyler Brule launched a magazine with shops, cafes and radio from day one. | Diversified revenue before it was fashionable: print subscriptions, retail, cafes, branded partnerships, a 24-hour radio station. | Never be one revenue line. |
| **Fever** | 2011, Madrid | A city events discovery app. | Turned discovery data into its own products (Candlelight concerts) and became an experiences marketplace. Valuation over US$1B, UNSURE on the exact figure. | Use what people search for to decide what experience to build. Then own the experience. This is the closest model to Vaya Experiences, and it started in Jordan's city. |
| **Nomad List** | 2014, remote | Pieter Levels posted a public spreadsheet asking nomads to rate cities on cost, internet and weather. | The spreadsheet became a site, the site became a paid membership community. Solo founder, profitable, no investors. | Crowdsource the data, charge for the community. The cheapest possible start. |
| **Morning Brew** | 2015, University of Michigan | Alex Lieberman's email summarizing business news for classmates, with Austin Rief. | Campus ambassadors, a referral program with merch rewards, sponsors from early on. Profitable before selling 75% to Insider in 2020 for US$75M. | Referral ladder from the first issue. Sponsors before subscriptions. |
| **The Athletic** | 2016, Chicago | Alex Mather and Adam Hansmann, ex-Strava, launched a subscription sports site in one city with journalists laid off by newspapers. | City by city, no ads, venture funded, sold to the New York Times in 2022 for US$550M. Only turned profitable inside the Times bundle. | One city, hire the local journalist the newspapers dropped, charge from day one. But watch the burn. |
| **BuzzFeed** | 2006, New York | Jonah Peretti's lab for what spreads on the internet. | Native ads on viral content, then a newsroom under Ben Smith from 2011. Ad model collapsed with the platforms. | The lesson is what not to do: distribution you don't own. |
| **Puck** | 2021, New York | Jon Kelly signed a handful of star reporters, each with their own newsletter and equity. | Premium sponsorships plus subscriptions, small paid summits. | Give the host equity. |
| **Semafor** | 2022, New York | Ben Smith and Justin Smith raised about US$25M and launched newsletters plus events. | Half ads, half events. Profitable by year three. | Events are the second product, not an afterthought. |
| **404 Media** | 2023, US | Four Motherboard journalists left Vice and launched a subscription site in weeks. | Scoops, one podcast, worker-owned. Profitable in six months. | Launch small, charge immediately, no office. |
| **Defector** | 2020, US | The Deadspin staff quit together and launched their own subscription site. | Loyal audience paid from day one. Flat at around US$4.6M a year since. | Loyalty pays salaries. It doesn't grow. |
| **Yes Theory** | 2015, Montreal | Four friends filmed a 30-day challenge of doing one uncomfortable thing a day. | YouTube growth, then their own apparel brand Seek Discomfort, then sponsors. | A format you can repeat every week, then merchandise the feeling. |
| **Johnny Harris** | 2019 to 2020, remote | Left Vox after hosting Borders and started his own channel. | His name carried the audience, not Vox's. Now a 30-person studio. | Trust attaches to a person. Vaya's host is the asset, protect them. |

### The ten moves they share

1. One city, or one niche, before a network. Eater, Thrillist, The Athletic, Vice all started in one place.
2. An owned channel first: an email list (Thrillist, Morning Brew) or a free print product (Vice, Time Out). The website came second.
3. Someone else's money paid for the free product: brands for Vice and Morning Brew. Or the audience paid from day one: The Athletic, Nomad List, 404.
4. Ride a platform's growth early and get off before it turns: Vice and Vox on YouTube, BuzzFeed on Facebook (which didn't get off).
5. Hire the person with the audience. Vox hired Klein and Topolsky. The Athletic hired the beat reporters newspapers cut.
6. A signature format people can name: the Vice Guide to Travel, the Vox explainer, the Eater 38, the Infatuation review.
7. Crowdsource what you can't afford to report: Nomad List's spreadsheet, SB Nation's fan bloggers.
8. A referral loop from the first issue: Morning Brew's ambassadors and merch ladder.
9. Own the real-world layer: Time Out Market, Monocle's shops, Fever's Candlelight, Yes Theory's apparel.
10. Know the exit: Morning Brew to Insider, Eater to Vox, The Athletic to the Times, The Infatuation to Chase. Vice's IPO never came.

## List 2. Vaya projects, revenue first, then the video

### The order

- **This week:** 2 and 3 (affiliates, forms).
- **First 30 days:** 1 (food tour), first version of 5 (investor report).
- **Days 30 to 60:** 4 (nights), 6 (brokers), 9 (first meetup).
- **Days 60 to 90:** 7 (sponsored week), 8 (first concierge client), then 12 (the films).
- **Later:** 10, 11.

- Jordan: signs the guide, the brokers, the sponsors, the affiliate programs, and the first company. Writes the investor report. Sells.
