There are **three different businesses** hiding inside the phrase "get into FDI," and they have wildly different licensing exposure.

| | Business | What you touch | Licence needed | Verdict |

| **B** | **Services to inbound investors.** Staffing, incorporation support, office, relocation, the people who actually run the Philippine entity. | Your own fees | Normal business licences | **You already own this. It is Placewell.** |
| **C** | **Capital.** Raising, pooling, placing, or advising on other people's investment money. | Other people's money | US broker-dealer or RIA, plus Philippine SEC | **Not before a lawyer and probably not at all.** |

**The whole game is staying in A and B while it looks from the outside like you are in C.** Investors do not actually want your money. They want to know whether the deal is real, who to trust on the ground, and who will run it. That is A and B, and it is the part nobody in Manila sells well.

**And the thing you keep missing: Placewell is already an FDI product.** Every American company setting up in the Philippines needs people before it needs anything else. You are already standing in the doorway. You do not need a fund.

## 1. What "FDI from the US to the Philippines" actually looks like

Four shapes, easiest to hardest:

| Shape | What it is | Who does it |
|---|---|---|
| **Portfolio** | Buying PSE-listed stock, bonds, funds from abroad | Anyone with a brokerage account. Not FDI, not your business. |
| **Real property** | A condo unit, a lease, a development | Retirees and small investors. The most common American "investment" in the Philippines. |
| **Operating business** | Setting up or buying a Philippine company: BPO, manufacturing, food, services | Corporates and serious founders. The real FDI. |
| **Private lending or equity into local businesses** | Funding a Filipino operator | Puhunan's territory. The most regulated of all. |

Your realistic audience, in order of how reachable they are: **Fil-Am families with money and a reason to care**, then **small US operators who want a Philippine back office**, then **retirees buying property**, then **institutional capital**, which you will not get and should not chase.

## 2. The Philippine side is easier than you think

The Philippines spent 2021 and 2022 liberalising hard, and most Americans do not know it.

**The core rule (RA 7042, the Foreign Investments Act, as amended by RA 11647 in 2022):** 100% foreign ownership of a Philippine company is allowed **unless** the Constitution or a specific law restricts it ([Cruz Marcelo](https://cruzmarcelo.com/republic-act-no-11647-amends-the-foreign-investment-act-of-1991-to-attract-foreign-investors/), [Platon Martinez](https://platonmartinez.com/articles/republic-act-no-11647-amendments-to-foreign-investments-act-fia-of-1991)).

**The money gate.** A foreign-owned company selling into the Philippine domestic market needs **US$200,000 minimum paid-in capital**. That drops to **US$100,000** if any one of these is true ([NDV Law](https://ndvlaw.com/the-usd-200000-capitalization-rule-setting-up-a-foreign-owned-domestic-corporation-the-right-way/), [Forvis Mazars](https://www.forvismazars.com/ph/en/insights/our-publications/doing-business-in-the-philippines-updates/amendments-to-foreign-investments-act)):

1. It uses advanced technology, as determined by the DOST
2. It is endorsed as a startup or startup enabler under RA 11337
3. **A majority of its direct employees are Filipino and there are at least 15 of them** (RA 11647 cut this from 50)

That third one is the door most people walk through, and **it is a hiring question, which is Placewell's business.** Write that down.

**Export enterprises** (60%+ of output exported) sit outside the domestic-market rule and can be 100% foreign-owned at ordinary corporate capital levels. UNSURE on the exact minimum paid-up figure; verify with the lawyer.

**What opened up recently:**
- **Telecoms, domestic shipping, airlines, railways, expressways and tollways**: removed from the constitutional definition of "public utility" by the Public Service Act amendment, RA 11659 (21 March 2022). Up to 100% foreign, with a reciprocity condition for telecoms ([PwC](https://www.pwc.com/ph/en/tax/issues/ra-11659-public-service-act.html), [NDV Law](https://ndvlaw.com/how-foreigners-can-own-100-of-philippine-telecom-and-transport-firms/)).
- **Retail trade**: minimum paid-up capital for a foreign retailer cut from US$2.5M to **PHP 25M** (about US$446,000) by RA 11595.
- **Renewable energy**: opened to full foreign ownership by DOJ opinion in 2022. UNSURE whether that has since been challenged; check.

**What is still closed, and one of them matters to you:**
- **Land. Full stop.** No foreigner owns Philippine land. Condominium *units* yes, but foreign membership in any condominium corporation is capped at **40% of that project** ([Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=165549fa-69c2-403f-bd38-f54284885f1f)). Long lease is the workaround: **50 years, renewable once for 25**, for industrial, tourism, commercial or development use.
- **Mass media: 100% Filipino, by the Constitution.** Vaya is a media company. This is already flagged as an open decision in `DECISIONS.md` and it gets sharper the moment Vaya adds an investment-facing arm. Ask the lawyer where the line sits between a media company and a company that publishes.
- Advertising is capped at 70% foreign. Practice of most licensed professions is Filipino-only.
- The rest is in the **Foreign Investment Negative List**, Lists A and B ([Triple i](https://www.tripleiconsulting.com/foreign-investment-negative-list/)). UNSURE whether a 13th FINL has superseded the 12th (EO 175, 2022). Confirm before publishing anything.

**The practical route in:** register with the SEC, then decide between **BOI** registration (incentives for priority activities, can be outside an economic zone) and **PEZA** (economic zone, stronger incentives, location-bound). CREATE and CREATE MORE reshaped the incentive menu. That choice alone is a paid briefing.

## 3. The US side is where the danger actually is

This is the part people get backwards. The Philippines will mostly let you in. **The SEC in Washington is what ends businesses like this.**

**The one rule that matters: never take a cut of the money.**

Transaction-based compensation, a percentage of capital raised, a success fee on a closed investment, is treated by US regulators as **near-conclusive evidence that you are acting as an unregistered broker-dealer** ([Nelson Mullins](https://www.nelsonmullins.com/insights/alerts/private-funds-and-investment-management-reports/all/private-funds-and-unregistered-finders-how-fund-sponsors-can-avoid-unnecessary-risk), [DarrowEverett](https://darroweverett.com/using-an-unregistered-broker-dealer-for-capital-raising-is-a-risky-proposition/), [Bean Kinney](https://www.beankinney.com/raising-money-paying-finders-fees-to-unregistered-broker-dealers-can-be-risky/)).

The consequence is worse than a fine. Under **Section 29(b) of the Exchange Act**, investors can seek **rescission**: they get to unwind the deal and demand their money back **if it goes badly**. So the downside is asymmetric and it lands exactly when you can least afford it. You carry the risk of every deal you touched, for years, with no upside.

The finder's exemption people talk about is narrow, contested, and it does not cover what you would actually be doing.

**If you ever pool money:** that is a securities offering. Regulation D 506(b) or 506(c), accredited investors, Form D filed, real subscription documents, and probably an RIA registration if you are advising for a fee. This is a lawyer-and-compliance-budget business, not a side product.

**And the tax reporting will scare your customers, so know it before they ask:**
- **Form 5471** is triggered at **10%** ownership of a foreign corporation, for shareholders, officers and directors ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-5471))
- If US persons control **more than 50%** for 30 days or more, it is a **Controlled Foreign Corporation**, which brings **GILTI** and Form 8992
- **FBAR** on foreign accounts over the threshold, plus FATCA
- Penalties on these are per-form and severe

**This is a content goldmine.** "The three IRS forms nobody tells you about before you buy a Philippine business" is a film, and its call to action is a paid briefing, not a deal.

## 5. Naming, which is a legal act here

**Do not call it Vaya Capital, Vaya Invest, Vaya Ventures or Vaya Fund.** Those names describe money management. A regulator reading your homepage does not care what you meant. The name should describe information and access, because that is what you are selling.

Per the naming system in `BRAND.md`: strands may be coined or thematic; services are Vaya plus a plain noun.

- **Strand (the content):** **Puhunan.** It is Tagalog for the money you start a business with. It covers capital moving in both directions, Filipino micro-entrepreneurs and American investors alike. It is the right word and you already own it.
- **Service, three options:**
  - *Safe:* **Vaya Desk**, matching Visa Desk. A counter you walk up to with a question. Says information, promises nothing financial.
  - *Bold:* **The Ground Check.** Names the one thing nobody else will do: go and physically look. Strongest differentiator, narrower scope.
  - *Weird:* **Due North.** Reads as due diligence without saying it. Memorable, but drifts toward finance, which is the direction you do not want to drift.

Recommendation: **Puhunan** as the strand, **Vaya Desk** as the service, **The Ground Check** as the flagship product inside it.

## 7. The top three ways this goes wrong

1. **You take a success fee.** Someone offers 2% of a US$500,000 deal for the introduction. It is US$10,000 and it is the end of the business: unregistered broker-dealer, and every investor you ever introduced gets a rescission right if their deal sours. **Flat fees only, invoiced before the outcome is known.**
2. **You are perceived as recommending an investment.** A film that says "this building is a good buy" and links to a partner is investment advice with an undisclosed interest, in a country where you are also selling services. The mitigation is the one you already built: the `standards.html` disclosure rule, applied to money the same way it is applied to restaurants.
3. **You publish a rule that has changed and someone loses money on it.** Philippine foreign-ownership rules moved three times between 2021 and 2022 and the negative list is periodically reissued. **Every published rule carries the date it was checked and the citation.** No exceptions. This is the single highest-liability content Vaya will ever publish.

A fourth, quieter one: the mass-media ownership restriction. Vaya is a media company advising foreigners on Philippine ownership rules while being foreign-owned itself. Get the lawyer's read on this before the FDI line has a public page.
